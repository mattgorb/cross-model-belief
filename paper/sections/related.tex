\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Correlated errors undermine oversight --- at the output level.}
That models fail together, and that this is a problem for oversight built on
agreement, is established and is not our claim.

\citet{goel2025alike} compare models' \emph{predictions} on benchmarks and define
CAPA, agreement on wrong answers corrected for the agreement two models would
reach by chance given their accuracies. With it they show three things: judges
score models similar to themselves more highly, weak-to-strong training gains
more when supervisor and student know different things, and similarity has been
rising across model generations. \citet{kim2025correlated} measure conditional
error agreement across more than $350$ models from two leaderboards and a
resume-screening task: when both models err they select the same wrong answer
roughly $60\%$ of the time, and this rises with accuracy rather than falling,
persisting across architectures and providers. \citet{ding2026agree} ask whether
agreement predicts correctness --- across a model's own samples and across a panel
of judges --- and find that it does not reliably.

All three read what models \emph{say}. We compare what two models
\emph{represent}, and the distinction is not a refinement: two models can reach
the same wrong answer by different internal routes, and output-level agreement
cannot tell those cases apart. Whether a probe-based checker escapes the problem
depends entirely on which case holds, so the question has to be asked at the
level of internal state.

The relationship to CAPA is worth being precise about, because our
$\rho/\rho_{\max}$ is the same \emph{kind} of statistic --- error overlap,
corrected for what the marginals force. The difference is that we obtain it by
decomposing the false-agreement rate exactly rather than by defining a measure
(Section~\ref{sec:theory}), and the decomposition does work a defined metric
cannot: it predicts, and then explains, the result that contradicted our own
hypothesis. Pairs from different families genuinely have lower error correlation,
but they carry higher individual error rates, and the identity's two terms cancel
so the rate does not move. A chance-adjusted metric can report that pattern; an
identity accounts for it.

\paragraph{Probes, truth directions, and their failure modes.}
\citet{burns2023ccs} recover a truth direction without supervision, and
\citet{marks2024geometry} show the direction is linear and causally relevant.
\citet{liu2023dissonance} examine where probes and outputs disagree and find
probes often the more accurate of the two, which is the premise a probe-based
checker needs. The method's limits are equally well documented:
\citet{farquhar2024challenges} show unsupervised discovery can lock onto
whichever salient binary feature the data offers rather than truth. We reproduce
that failure --- our unsupervised probe reads truth on curated factual statements
and not on adversarial misconceptions --- and it is why the probes we rely on are
supervised. Truth directions are also known not to transfer freely across
\emph{tasks} and logical forms within a single model
\citep{bao2025probing}, which is the within-model analogue of the
across-dataset transfer we measure.

Closest in spirit, \citet{pandey2026sycophancy} read internal state rather than
outputs, and find the same small set of attention heads carrying a ``this
statement is wrong'' signal across twelve open-weight models from five labs. A
shared internal signal across models is therefore observed rather than
hypothesised, which is prior evidence for the mechanism behind our result. Their
question is sycophancy --- does a model notice that the \emph{user's} claim is
false and agree anyway --- so the comparison is one model against its
interlocutor. Ours is one model's readout against another's, and whether their
joint failure leaves a signature.

\paragraph{Transporting probes between models.}
Reusing a probe characterized on one model requires a map between activation
spaces. \citet{chen2025stitching} fit affine maps between residual streams and
transfer sparse autoencoders, probes and steering vectors across model sizes;
\citet{oozeer2025interventions} transfer safety interventions through learned
mappings across Llama, Qwen and Gemma. The counter-evidence matters too:
\citet{zhang2026negative} report that a map can align representations well under
an offline objective while the translated states remain unusable causally inside
the receiver. Our Gate~B measures what transfer costs a probe in the only unit
that matters for oversight --- discriminative performance lost --- and we report
it per dataset rather than as a single verdict, because it ranges from almost
nothing to total failure.

\paragraph{Representational convergence, and what it licenses.}
The platonic representation hypothesis \citep{huh2024platonic} holds that
independently trained models converge toward a shared representation as they
scale, and it is the premise on which cross-model probing rests: without some
such convergence there is no reason a probe fitted on one model should mean
anything read on another. The theoretical form of the claim is sharper.
Identifiability results for discriminative models imply that two models inducing
the same conditional distribution have representations equal up to an invertible
linear transformation, and \citet{nielsen2026logit} ask what survives when the
distributions are merely close rather than equal. Their answer matters for us
twice over. Closeness in a logit-based distance does yield linear similarity
guarantees, which is the licence for using a linear map at all. But closeness in
KL does \emph{not}: two models can match each other's predictions while failing to
preserve linear-probe recoverability of the same concepts. Output agreement is
therefore not evidence of representational agreement, which is the gap the
output-level studies above cannot see into and this paper measures directly.

\paragraph{Transporting probes between models.}
Several lines of work fit maps between activation spaces and move something
through them. \citet{gorbett2026ventriloquist} learn affine maps between the
final hidden states of independent models and find downstream performance largely
preserved --- decision boundaries and confidence structure both --- with linear
alignment sometimes sufficient to decode one model's states through another's
frozen output head. Three of their findings bound what a transported probe can be
said to measure, and we adopt all three. Compatibility localizes to the final one
or two layers, making it a late-stage, output-space phenomenon rather than shared
reasoning; transfer is asymmetric, with strong-to-weak mappings outperforming
weak-to-strong and source capacity the limiting factor; and tokenizer overlap
predicts success. We observe the same asymmetry in a different task, in the
direction their account predicts.

\citet{chen2025stitching} fit affine maps between residual streams and transfer
sparse autoencoders, probes and steering vectors across model sizes;
\citet{oozeer2025interventions} transfer safety interventions through learned
mappings across Llama, Qwen and Gemma. The counter-evidence matters as much:
\citet{zhang2026negative} report a map aligning representations well under an
offline objective while the translated states remain unusable causally inside the
receiver. Our transport measurement is deliberately in the units that decide the
question for oversight --- discriminative performance lost by the probe --- and we
report it per dataset and per direction, because it ranges from almost nothing to
total failure.

CKA \citep{kornblith2019similarity} is the standard measure of whether two
networks represent inputs alike, and we report it as context rather than as the
verdict on transportability. A probe needs one direction to survive where CKA
grades all of them, and the two come apart: we observe a pair at CKA $0.49$ whose
transported probe loses $0.004$ AUROC. CKA does predict transfer loss moderately
across our cells ($r=-0.51$ against the worse of the two directions). The
quantity \citet{nielsen2026logit} identify would be the better-motivated
predictor; a version of it computable from our cache does not beat CKA, for
reasons that are about our proxy rather than their result
(Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:logit}).

\paragraph{Cross-model disagreement as a signal.}
The complement of what we study has been shown to work.
\citet{gorbett2026disagreement} use a second model's surprise at the first's
answer as a label-free correctness signal, requiring one forward pass and no
generation from the verifier, and report substantially better detection of
confident errors than within-model uncertainty baselines. That establishes that
cross-model \emph{disagreement} carries correctness information in output space.
This paper asks the opposite question --- what cross-model \emph{agreement}
misses --- and moves the measurement into representation space. The free coverage
in our operating-point analysis is that signal, read from probe verdicts rather
than from token surprise, and the comparison between the two spaces is one of our
gates.

\paragraph{Scalable oversight.}
Debate \citep{irving2018debate}, sandwiching \citep{bowman2022measuring} and
weak-to-strong training \citep{burns2023weak} all place models in the checking
role, and \citet{panickssery2024selfpreference} show judges recognize and favour
their own generations, one concrete mechanism by which a checker's errors
correlate with the checked model's. On the theoretical side,
\citet{afrin2026ensembles} analyse evaluator ensembles under optimization
pressure, decompose the error covariance, and prove that the common-mode
component is not identifiable from the judges' own scores --- disagreement reveals
only the orthogonal part. Our empirical finding, that the shared blind spot is
barely detectable from the activations that produced it, is the
representation-level analogue of that theorem, reached from the other direction:
they show the shared component is invisible in the scores, we measure how little
of it is visible in the states.

\paragraph{What is new here.}
Three things, stated narrowly. (i) The measurement is of internal readouts rather
than outputs, which is the only level at which the question above can be settled,
and no prior work measures agreement between two models' probes. (ii) The rate is
derived rather than defined, giving an upper bound set by the better probe's own
error rate and an explanation for why family diversity does not help. (iii) Two
experiments have no counterpart in the work above: the cost of transporting a
probe between models, measured in discriminative performance lost and reported
per dataset and per direction; and the detectability of the blind spot, where a
direction is fitted on the false agreements of four datasets and tested on a
fifth.

\paragraph{Ensemble diversity.}
The dependence of ensemble error on the covariance between members is classical
\citep{brown2005diversity}, and the correlation floor we state for $N$ overseers
is a restatement of it rather than a new result. The constraint that a joint
distribution's marginals impose on how much two binary errors can overlap is
older still \citep{frechet1951tableaux}. Our use of both is to turn them on
probe-based oversight and to report where the measured quantities sit inside the
bounds they imply.
