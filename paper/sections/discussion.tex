\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Implications for model-judge selection.}
Accuracy alone does not determine whether a second model adds useful oversight.
What matters is whether its errors overlap those of the model being checked.
Model family is not a sufficient proxy for that overlap. Cross-family pairs
have less-correlated errors in aggregate, but the difference disappears among
similarly sized models and does not reliably reduce false agreement. Judge
selection should therefore measure both individual accuracy and joint error
directly, on data representative of deployment.

\paragraph{An output-level reference point.}
For scale, the released pair-level results of \citet{kim2025correlated} imply
that two models give the same wrong output on an average of $9.2\%$ of HELM
questions and $17.7\%$ of Hugging Face leaderboard questions. The corresponding
rate for our logistic-probe readouts is $7.9\%$. Relative to the union of each
pair's errors, overlap is $24.2\%$ and $26.7\%$ in their output-level data,
compared with $31.3\%$ for our internal readouts. These figures are not directly
matched across models or tasks, but show that shared internal-readout error is
of the same order as previously measured output error.

\paragraph{The residual blind spot.}
False agreement is detectable in-domain, showing that shared errors can leave a
linear signature in model activations. Its uneven transfer to unseen data
distributions, however, argues against a universal detector fitted once and
reused without validation. A more realistic use is domain-specific triage, where
disagreement supplies a first routing signal, and a detector trained on
representative labeled data prioritizes additional review among the claims on
which both probes agree.

\paragraph{Scope and limitations.}
Our results concern supervised probe readouts, not beliefs in an attributional
sense. A false agreement may reflect a misconception shared by both models, a
failure mode shared by both probes, or both; the present experiments do not
separate these explanations. The study also reduces model behavior to binary
truth judgments over five datasets, and the model-pair cells reuse models and
items rather than forming independent observations. Finally, the broad
leave-one-dataset-out analysis is stronger than the available in-domain
evidence, which currently covers only selected model pairs. These limitations
narrow the claim but not the measured blind spot. Agreement between two internal
readouts can conceal substantial shared error, and neither model accuracy nor
family diversity alone characterizes it.
