\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Two kinds of objects are fitted: a \emph{probe} for each model and a
\emph{direction} that scores false agreement for each model pair. Each is
evaluated on data excluded from its fit.

\subsection{The probe}
\label{sec:method:probe}

For a model and a claim we build a contrast pair: the claim followed by
\texttt{Is this claim true? Yes} and the same prefix followed by \texttt{No}. The
two halves differ in a single token, which is deliberate. The difference
between them is then a statement about the claim rather than about the surface
form. We read the final layer's mean-pooled hidden state for each half. The final layer
is the main operating point; additional experiments vary the layer and pooling
choice.

We compare three linear probes on identical activations, differing only in how
the direction is chosen:

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep0em
\item \textbf{Mass-mean}, the difference of class means, which
  \citet{marks2024geometry} show is the causally faithful direction.
\item \textbf{Logistic regression} on the same features, the standard
  linear-probing baseline.
\item \textbf{CCS} \citep{burns2023ccs}, unsupervised, fitted by the
  consistency-plus-confidence objective.
\end{itemize}

Two implementation points matter enough to state, because getting either wrong
produces a probe that looks healthy and reads nothing. First, CCS requires each
half of the contrast pair to be standardized \emph{separately}. The halves differ
by one token, so their difference contains a large constant direction, namely
the token identity, measured at roughly $1.7\times$ the item-to-item variation.
A shared normalizer leaves it in place. CCS then drives both loss terms to zero by
reading that direction alone, giving $p^+ \approx 1$ and $p^- \approx 0$ on
every item. The result is perfectly consistent, perfectly confident, and
uninformative. Second, and for the same reason, restarts cannot be ranked by
training loss, because the
degenerate solution attains a lower loss ($\sim\!10^{-4}$) than any probe that
tracks truth ($\sim\!10^{-2}$). We reject restarts whose belief output has
near-zero spread across items, which is a label-free criterion, before ranking the
survivors.

\paragraph{Does the probe read truth, or confidence?}
A probe that merely re-encoded the model's stated confidence would be useless for
oversight, since one could read the output instead. We therefore score every
probe on the half of items the model is \emph{most} confident about, against the
model's own signed confidence on the same items. Confidently wrong items are the
ones oversight has to catch, and a probe earns its place only by beating
confidence there.

\subsection{The false-agreement direction}
\label{sec:method:direction}

Within the subset where both probes read ``true'', we fit a linear classifier to
separate the genuinely true claims from the false ones. The feature space
concatenates, for both models, the contrast \emph{difference} and the contrast
\emph{mean} in their native activation spaces. The mean is kept deliberately
because a confound shared by both halves of a pair survives in the mean and
cancels in the difference, and that confound is what false agreement is partly
made of. A difference-only space would be engineered not to show it.

Two controls accompany every number. A \textbf{permutation control} refits the
direction on shuffled labels; because the feature space has tens of thousands of
columns, a direction fitted on noise is effectively a random projection and its
AUROC has a wide distribution, so we take several permutations and report the
spread. A margin smaller than that spread is not evidence. An
\textbf{output-confidence baseline} asks whether the activation space is needed
at all.

\subsection{Label-free alternatives to the third stage}
\label{sec:method:unsup}

The direction of Section~\ref{sec:method:direction} is fitted on labels: it needs
to know which of the agreed-upon claims were false. Where those labels come from
decides what the method can claim.

Three situations should be kept apart. A detector fitted and scored on the same
dataset needs labels \emph{on the distribution being overseen} --- which, in the
setting that motivates this work, is precisely what is unavailable: if the claims
could be checked, the oversight problem would not arise. The
leave-one-dataset-out protocol removes that requirement, fitting the direction on
other datasets and applying it to an unlabelled target; labels are still needed,
but elsewhere. The third situation needs none at all, and is the subject of this
section.

The first two stages remain supervised throughout. Each model's probe is fitted
once, on whatever labelled data is available, and that cost is paid once per
model rather than once per deployment. The question here is only whether the
\emph{third} stage can be had for free, given two probes that already exist.

We consider three scores. None is fitted on labels; labels are used only to
compute the reported AUROC afterwards.

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep0em
\item \textbf{Margin.} How weakly the more hesitant of the two probes agreed:
  $-\min(|b_1 - \tfrac12|,\, |b_2 - \tfrac12|)$, where $b_i$ is probe $i$'s
  belief. This requires no fitting of any kind --- it is arithmetic on the two
  probes' own outputs, so a system that has the probes already has this score.
  It tests whether agreement reached near the decision boundary is less
  trustworthy than confident agreement.
\item \textbf{Anomaly.} Mahalanobis distance from the centre of the
  both-agree pool, in a basis of its top $32$ principal components. The
  projection and covariance are estimated on the unlabelled pool itself. It
  tests whether shared mistakes occupy unusual regions of the joint
  representation rather than typical ones. Unlike the margin, this reads the
  activations of both models, not only the probes' scalar outputs.
\item \textbf{Spread.} The gap between the two probes' beliefs,
  $|b_1 - b_2|$, among claims where both nonetheless say true. It tests whether
  residual disagreement inside an agreement carries a warning.
\end{itemize}

We do not use CCS here. Its objective is defined over a claim and its negation,
and the quantity to be separated at this stage is not a claim's truth but
whether an \emph{agreement} is mistaken; constructing the corresponding
contrastive pair would require a second extraction over prompts about the
agreement itself, which we do not have. CCS appears in this paper only as an
alternative for the first two stages (Section~\ref{sec:method:probe}).

A caveat on reporting. Choosing the best of the three scores per cell would
itself consume labels, so that quantity is an upper bound rather than a method,
and we report each score separately.

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:method:protocol}

Splits are group-aware, so a true/false pair built from one source question never
straddles the train/test boundary. Within a dataset, probes are fitted on
the train split and everything is scored on the test split.

The generalization test is leave-one-dataset-out. For each held-out dataset the
probes are fitted on the other datasets' train splits and the direction on their
test splits, so the direction never learns from items whose verdicts its own
probes could have memorized, and the held-out dataset is unseen by both stages.
One detail is necessary rather than cosmetic. A probe keeps
its direction across datasets but not its threshold, because activation scale
shifts and the fitted boundary lands in the wrong place. Left uncalibrated this
produced zero agreements on one dataset and $99.7\%$ disagreement on another,
invisible in AUROC, which is threshold-free, but fatal to a subset defined by a
threshold. We therefore hold each probe's own positive rate fixed at its value
where it was fitted and read the threshold off the deployment scores, which needs
no labels.

\paragraph{Models and data.}
Sixteen open-weight models spanning five families and $1.7$B to $405$B
parameters give $571$ unique model-pair--dataset cells. We use five datasets:
curated true/false statements \citep{marks2024geometry}, TruthfulQA
\citep{lin2022truthfulqa}, BoolQ \citep{clark2019boolq}, RTE
\citep{wang2018glue} and IMDB \citep{maas2011imdb}. Activations are extracted in
\texttt{fp16} and cached, so every analysis downstream is deterministic and needs
no accelerator.

\paragraph{Reasoning models.}
All but one family are hybrid thinking models. We extract with no chat template
and thinking off, so no reasoning trace is generated. This keeps the comparison
identical across reasoning and non-reasoning models and keeps the output-space
baseline a genuine single-token Yes/No logprob, which it would not be if the next
token began a trace. The probe therefore reads \emph{pre-reasoning} belief, and we
say so rather than calling it belief.

\paragraph{What we do not separate.}
False agreement has two possible causes: the probes faithfully report a belief the
two models share and that belief is wrong, or both probes fail in the same way,
tracking familiarity rather than truth. Distinguishing them requires an
attribution control that recovers a prominence direction from familiar versus
defamiliarized phrasings of the same claim and testing whether false agreements lie
along it. We have not run this control. Our claims are therefore about the agreement of
probe readouts, and we are explicit where that is weaker than a claim about the
models themselves.
