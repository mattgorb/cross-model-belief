\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

One language model checking another model's output is now a default instrument
of AI oversight. LLM judges grade benchmarks, filter pretraining corpora, supply
reward signals, and watch deployed behaviour. The appeal is obvious: models are
cheap, fast, and available in quantity, while human annotators are not
\citep{zheng2023judge}. Other oversight schemes, including debate
\citep{irving2018debate}, sandwiching \citep{bowman2022measuring}, and
weak-to-strong training \citep{burns2023weak}, also rely on models checking or
supervising other models. All depend on the check remaining informative when
the model being checked is wrong.

Every such approach rests on an assumption that is almost never tested: the
checker and the model being checked must fail differently. Output-based
checking, however, has two potential failure modes. The checker cannot assess
what the model does not express in its generated response, and it cannot catch
errors shared by both models.

The first blindness has a known answer. Linear probes read a model's internal
state rather than its output, recovering a direction along which true and false
claims separate \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2024geometry}. The probe literature has
established that these directions exist, that they can be more accurate than the
model's own stated answer \citep{liu2023dissonance}, and that the unsupervised
version of the method is fragile in specific ways
\citep{farquhar2024challenges}. A probe therefore gives a checker access to what
the model represents rather than what it chose to say.

Whether probes answer the second blindness is a different question, and it is the
one we ask. The output-level version has been settled, and the answer is
discouraging: model mistakes are substantially correlated, more so as models get
more capable, and this undermines oversight schemes built on agreement
\citep{goel2025alike,kim2025correlated}. But correlated \emph{outputs} do not
have to mean correlated \emph{representations}. Two models might converge on the
same answer through different internal routes, in which case a reader of
internal state would see the disagreement an output-level judge misses.

\paragraph{False agreement.}
We fit a probe on each of two models, read both on the same claim, and ask how
often they are both wrong in the same direction: both read a claim as true when
the claim is false. We call this \emph{false agreement}. It is the case
disagreement-based oversight cannot see. The two readouts match, nothing is
flagged, and the answer is wrong.

How much of it there can be is settled in advance by two quantities: how often
each probe is wrong, and how much those two sets of mistakes overlap. Neither is
sufficient on its own. A pair of probes can be individually excellent and still
hide most of a model's errors if they are wrong on the same items, and no amount
of accuracy in either one repairs that; the overlap term is simply added on top.
Section~\ref{sec:theory} makes this exact and derives the ceiling. The blind spot
cannot be larger than the better probe's own error rate, and cannot be driven
below the product of the two rates except through anti-correlated mistakes.

\paragraph{What we test.}
We go beyond asking whether shared error persists in internal readouts in three
ways. First, we decompose false agreement into the probes' individual error
rates and their error correlation, showing how greater independence can be
offset by lower accuracy. Second, we test whether choosing models from different
families reduces this blind spot after accounting for model size and probe
estimator. Third, we ask whether false agreement can itself be detected, fitting
a classifier to distinguish false from genuine agreement and evaluating it both
in-domain and on unseen data distributions.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
The study covers $571$ unique model-pair--dataset cells formed from $16$
open-weight models across five families, spanning $1.7$B to $405$B parameters,
on five datasets and with two supervised probe estimators. It makes four
contributions.

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep0em
\item \textbf{A measurable account of the blind spot, and a broad measurement of
  it.} We define false agreement for two internal readouts and decompose it
  exactly into the two probes' individual false-positive rates and the
  correlation between their errors; Fr\'echet bounds locate its feasible range
  and show why accuracy alone cannot characterize it. Measured across $571$
  unique model-pair--dataset cells --- $16$ models from five families, $1.7$B to
  $405$B parameters, five datasets, two probe estimators --- false agreement
  averages $2$--$26\%$ of false claims, and probe errors are positively
  correlated in $84$--$98\%$ of cells depending on the probe, placing every
  dataset above the rate two independent probes would reach
  (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}A).
\item \textbf{A negative result for model-family diversity.}
  With logistic probes, false agreement is $12.5\%$ within families and
  $13.2\%$ across them; with mass-mean probes, it is $11.7\%$ and $11.2\%$.
  Neither difference is distinguishable from zero, and the sign reverses between
  probes. Cross-family errors are less correlated in aggregate, falling from
  $0.42$ to $0.34$ with logistic probes and from $0.32$ to $0.24$ with mass-mean
  probes. Among models with a size ratio below $1.5$, however, the within- and
  cross-family correlations are instead $0.389$ and $0.406$ for logistic probes,
  and $0.283$ and $0.286$ for mass-mean probes: the aggregate reduction reflects
  the size gap between families, not diversity itself. Model family therefore
  does not reliably identify a judge with a smaller shared-error blind spot
  (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}).
\item \textbf{A test of whether the blind spot can be detected.}
  Evaluated within a dataset, false agreement separates from genuine agreement
  at a mean AUROC of $0.81$, and every one of the $446$ scorable cells beats
  both chance and its own permutation control. The operating point is the
  concrete form of this: on curated factual statements, reviewing the
  top-ranked $10\%$ of the claims on which both probes say true recovers
  $78\%$ of the false agreements among them. Under leave-one-dataset-out
  transfer the same budget recovers $45$--$48\%$ there and only $9$--$20\%$ on
  the other four datasets. False agreement is detectable, but its signature is
  largely domain-specific.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Together, these results make shared error an explicit quantity for
evaluating model judges rather than an assumption inferred from accuracy or
model family.
