\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

One language model checking another model’s output is a default instrument of AI
oversight. Judges grade benchmarks, filter pretraining corpora, supply reward
signals, and watch deployed behaviour. The appeal is obvious: models are cheap,
fast, and available in quantity, unlike human annotators
\citep{zheng2023judge}. Other oversight schemes, including debate
\citep{irving2018debate}, sandwiching \citep{bowman2022measuring}, and
weak-to-strong training \citep{burns2023weak}, also rely on models checking or
supervising other models. All depend on the checker catching errors when the
model being checked is wrong.

Every such approach rests on an assumption that is almost never tested: the
checker and the model being checked must fail differently. However, output-based
checking has two potential failure modes: the checker cannot assess what the
model does not express, and it cannot catch errors shared by both models.

The first blindness has a known answer. Linear probes read a model's internal
state rather than its output, recovering a direction along which true and false
claims separate \citep{burns2023ccs,marks2024geometry}. The probe literature has
established that these directions exist, that they can be more accurate than the
model's own stated answer \citep{liu2023dissonance}, and that the unsupervised
version of the method is fragile in specific ways
\citep{farquhar2024challenges}. A probe therefore gives a checker access to what
the model represents rather than what it chose to say.

Whether probes answer the second blindness is a different question, and it is the
one we ask. The output-level version has been settled, and the answer is
discouraging: model mistakes are substantially correlated, more so as models get
more capable, and this undermines oversight schemes built on agreement
\citep{goel2025alike,kim2025correlated}. But correlated \emph{outputs} do not
have to mean correlated \emph{representations}. Two models might converge on the
same answer through different internal routes, in which case a reader of
internal state would see the disagreement an output-level judge misses.

\paragraph{False agreement.}
We fit a probe on each of two models, read both on the same claim, and ask how
often they are both wrong in the same direction: both read a claim as true when
the claim is false. We call this \emph{false agreement}. It is the case
disagreement-based oversight cannot see --- the two readouts match, nothing is
flagged, and the answer is wrong.

How much of it there can be is settled in advance by two quantities: how often
each probe is wrong, and how much those two sets of mistakes overlap. Neither is
sufficient on its own. A pair of probes can be individually excellent and still
hide most of a model's errors if they are wrong on the same items, and no amount
of accuracy in either one repairs that --- the overlap term is simply added on top.
Section~\ref{sec:theory} makes this exact and derives the ceiling: the blind spot
cannot be larger than the better probe's own error rate, and cannot be driven
below the product of the two rates except by anti-correlated mistakes, which
models trained on overlapping corpora do not have.

\paragraph{Why a linear map, and why it cuts both ways.}
Reading one model's probe on another requires putting the two activation spaces
in correspondence, and the choice of a \emph{linear} map is not a convenience.
For discriminative models including autoregressive language models,
identifiability results imply that two models inducing the same conditional
distribution have representations equal up to an invertible linear transformation
\citep{nielsen2026logit}; a linear map is the object the theory says should
suffice. Empirically the same conclusion arrives from the other direction. The
platonic representation hypothesis holds that independently trained models
converge toward a shared representation as they scale \citep{huh2024platonic},
and affine maps between the late hidden states of independent models do preserve
decision boundaries and confidence structure well enough that one model's states
can sometimes be decoded by another's frozen output head
\citep{gorbett2026ventriloquist}.

That convergence is what makes this paper's method possible, and it is also what
makes the method's failure predictable. If two models come to represent the world
the same way, they are wrong about the same things: the hypothesis that licenses
reading model $A$'s probe on model $B$ predicts that the two readouts will share
a blind spot. Both halves of that tension are measurable, and we measure both.

Two cautions follow, and we carry them through the paper rather than relegating
them. First, the compatibility that linear maps exploit has been localized to the
final one or two layers --- a late-stage, output-space phenomenon rather than
evidence of shared reasoning \citep{gorbett2026ventriloquist} --- so what a
transported probe reads is a late-stage representation, and we say that rather
than claiming access to belief. Second, closeness of output distributions does not
imply linear representational similarity: KL divergence is a poor control on the
quantity that governs whether a probe survives a map, which is why a pair of
models can agree in their outputs while differing in the structure a probe reads
\citep{nielsen2026logit}. That gap is precisely the room in which an
internal-state measurement can say something an output-level one cannot.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
Our subject is a pair of models and the two readouts taken from them, scored
against ground truth to find the items on which both are wrong in the same
direction. The study covers $49$ ordered pairs drawn from
ten open-weight models across four families and more than an order of magnitude of
scale, on five datasets, with the overseer never larger than the model it
oversees. Four contributions follow.

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep0em
\item \textbf{An exact decomposition of the blind spot}
  (Section~\ref{sec:theory}), with an upper bound fixed by the more accurate
  probe's own error rate. It is what tells us the quantity to select for is not
  accuracy, and it is what later explains a result we did not expect.
\item \textbf{The measurement at the level of internal belief}
  (Section~\ref{sec:results}). False agreement is common, and the two probes'
  errors are positively correlated in nearly every pair: the blind spot is a
  property of what these models represent, not only of what they say.
\item \textbf{A negative result on diversity.} Drawing the two models from
  different families does not reduce false agreement. The decomposition accounts
  for it: the gain in independence is paid for in accuracy, and the two effects
  offset almost exactly. The obvious remedy is not one.
\item \textbf{The cost of transport, and the limit of self-diagnosis.} A probe
  validated on one model is only useful on another if it survives the map between
  them, so we measure what the trip costs in discriminative performance rather
  than assuming it is free --- it ranges from negligible to total failure across
  datasets. We then ask whether the shared error that constitutes the blind spot
  leaves a signature a classifier can learn, and find that it largely does not,
  except where false agreement is already rare. This is the representation-level
  counterpart of a result proved for judge scores, that common-mode error is not
  identifiable from the judges' own outputs \citep{afrin2026ensembles}.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Together these bound what agreement between two models' internal
beliefs can deliver, and locate the limit in shared error rather than in the
accuracy of either checker.
