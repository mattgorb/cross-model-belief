\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The probes read truth where it matters}

Gate~A asks whether a probe beats the model's own stated confidence on the half
of items the model is most confident about. On TruthfulQA, where $42\%$ of
confident items are answered wrongly, \emph{every} model passes. The probe adds
between $0.05$ and $0.35$ AUROC over confidence. On the remaining datasets the
models are usually right when confident, confidence is therefore already a good
predictor, and the probe's advantage is smaller and sometimes absent. The
instrument works where there is work for it to do, and TruthfulQA is accordingly
where the oversight questions below are sharpest.

The unsupervised probe does not survive this test. CCS reads truth on curated
factual statements at $0.99$ AUROC and fails on adversarial misconceptions,
scoring below the confidence baseline on the confident slice. This reproduces
the failure mode \citet{farquhar2024challenges} predict. All results below therefore
use a supervised probe in the primary analysis. A separate CCS base-model
stress test appears below; it uses an unsupervised belief probe but retains a
supervised false-agreement detector. In the primary pipeline, both fitted
components use labelled data: the probes on each model's training split, and the
false-agreement direction on the pooled false agreements of the training datasets. What the
leave-one-dataset-out protocol establishes is therefore supervised transfer to
an \emph{unlabelled target distribution} --- no labels are needed from the
dataset being overseen, and target labels are used only to score the result
afterwards. The oversight claim is ``fit once on labelled data, deploy on
unlabelled data'', not label-free detection.

\subsection{False agreement, and where it sits inside its bound}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig1_bound}
\caption{False agreement against the interval the marginals permit. The track
runs from the rate two probes would hit by coincidence alone ($p_1p_2$) to the
ceiling set by the more accurate probe's own error rate ($\min(p_1,p_2)$);
the dots are the measured rates for logistic and mass-mean probes, averaged over
all model pairs. Both ends depend on the probes'
own false-positive rates, so the two probe families have different intervals on
the same dataset: a more accurate probe gets a lower and tighter one. On
Geometry of Truth the logistic probe is the more accurate of the two and its
interval sits roughly four times lower; on BoolQ the ordering reverses.}
\label{fig:bound}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:bound} places the measured rate inside the interval
Section~\ref{sec:theory} derives. Across dataset averages, false agreement ranges
from $1.5\%$ to $25.5\%$ with logistic probes and from $4.7\%$ to $22.2\%$ with
mass-mean probes. It exceeds the independence reference on every dataset.
Errors are positively correlated in $98\%$ and $84\%$ of the $571$
model-pair--dataset cells, respectively.

\paragraph{Family diversity does not reduce it.}
The obvious remedy fails, and the decomposition says why. With logistic probes,
false agreement is $12.5\%$ within families and $13.2\%$ across them; with
mass-mean probes, it is $11.7\%$ and $11.2\%$. Neither difference is
distinguishable from zero, and its sign reverses across probes.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig2_diversity}
\caption{The cross-family minus within-family difference. Cross-family pairing
does not reduce false agreement under either probe. Error correlation is lower
across families in the aggregate, but not when model sizes are matched within a
ratio of $1.5$.}
\label{fig:diversity}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:diversity} separates the two terms. Cross-family error
correlation is lower by $0.078$ with logistic probes and $0.085$ with mass-mean
probes. Among similarly sized models, the differences reverse to $+0.017$ and
$+0.003$. The aggregate decorrelation therefore does not identify a robust
family effect, and the absolute blind spot does not shrink.

\subsection{Is the blind spot detectable?}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3_detect}
\caption{Leave-one-dataset-out detection by dataset and probe. Left: AUROC for
ranking false above genuine agreement. Right: the fraction of all false
agreements recovered by reviewing the top-ranked $10\%$ of claims on which both
probes say true. Cells with fewer than $20$ false agreements in the held-out
split are excluded, since AUROC is not meaningful on a handful of positives;
this removes $13$ of $128$ cells for geometry of truth under the mass-mean probe
and $92$ of $128$ for RTE under the logistic probe, whose point is therefore the
least well supported in the figure.}
\label{fig:detect}
\end{figure}

If false agreement is made of shared error, it might leave a signature.
Figure~\ref{fig:detect} tests whether that signature survives an unseen data
distribution. The mass-mean detector performs best, reaching AUROC $0.91$ on
Geometry of Truth and $0.72$ on RTE, but only $0.56$--$0.63$ elsewhere.
Logistic-probe results are weaker and remain near chance on IMDB and BoolQ.

The operating point makes the limitation concrete. Reviewing the top-ranked
$10\%$ recovers $45$--$48\%$ of false agreements on Geometry of Truth, but only
$9$--$20\%$ on the other four datasets. The signature is strong on curated
factual statements and largely domain-specific elsewhere.

\subsection{Detectability and transferability are separate properties}
\label{sec:indomain}

The leave-one-dataset-out result conflates two questions: whether shared error
leaves a linear signature at all, and whether that signature generalizes beyond
the distribution it was fitted on. Fitting and scoring the detector inside a
single dataset separates them. For each model pair and dataset we fit the
probes on the dataset's training rows as before, then score the detector by
five-fold cross-validation over the remaining rows, so that no claim is ranked
by a direction that has seen it.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig5_indomain}
\caption{Detection inside a dataset (filled) against the same dataset held out
of every stage of fitting (hollow); the arrow is the transfer cost.
\textbf{(A)} Ranking performance. \textbf{(B)} The fraction of false agreements
recovered by reviewing the top-ranked $10\%$ of claims on which both probes say
true. Cells with fewer than $20$ false agreements in the evaluated split are
excluded. Geometry of Truth and RTE lose almost nothing in transfer; IMDB,
BoolQ and TruthfulQA lose most of what they had. Mass-mean probes shown;
logistic probes are uniformly weaker as detectors
(Section~\ref{sec:indomain}).}
\label{fig:indomain}
\end{figure}

The signature exists, and it is not marginal. Across the $446$ cells with at
least $20$ false agreements to score, the mass-mean detector reaches a mean
AUROC of $0.812$, and \emph{every one} of them beats both chance and its own
permutation control. The strongest operating point in the study is here: on
Geometry of Truth, reviewing the top-ranked $10\%$ of the claims on which both
probes say true recovers $77.5\%$ of the false agreements among them.

What varies across datasets is not whether the signature exists but whether it
travels. Figure~\ref{fig:indomain} shows the two measurements together.
Geometry of Truth and IMDB are close in-domain, at $0.926$ and $0.887$, yet
Geometry of Truth retains $96\%$ of its lift above chance when the detector is
fitted elsewhere while IMDB retains $33\%$ --- falling from $0.887$ to $0.628$,
and from $54\%$ to $16\%$ of false agreements recovered at the same review
budget. BoolQ and TruthfulQA behave like IMDB, retaining $27\%$ and $41\%$.
Detectability and transferability are therefore distinct properties of a
dataset, and a detector that works well where it was fitted is not evidence that
it will work anywhere else.

The two probe families separate here as they do under transfer, and in the same
direction. Paired on the cells both can score, the logistic detector is
$0.070$ AUROC below the mass-mean detector ($t=-7.1$), winning on $21\%$ of
cells. The probe that reads belief more accurately is the weaker detector of
shared error, in-domain and out of it. Part of this is mechanical: a more
accurate probe produces fewer false agreements, so only $56\%$ of logistic cells
clear the twenty-positive threshold against $73\%$ of mass-mean cells, and the
positives that remain are the ones a good probe still misses. We report both
probes rather than selecting the stronger one.

\subsection{What an operator gets}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig4_pairs}
\caption{Share of all false claims caught by dataset and probe. The solid
segment is exposed by disagreement between the two probes. The hatched segment
is added by reviewing the detector's top-ranked $10\%$ of claims on which both
probes say true.}
\label{fig:pairs}
\end{figure}

Putting the pieces together as a deployment (Figure~\ref{fig:pairs}), two probes
read a claim, disagreement routes it for an expensive check, and a budget of
further checks is spent on the agreeing items the detector ranks highest.
Disagreement alone catches $29$--$45\%$ of false claims across datasets and
probes. A $10\%$ review budget raises total coverage to $33$--$50\%$, with the
largest gain on Geometry of Truth. On TruthfulQA, total coverage is $42\%$ with
logistic probes and $40\%$ with mass-mean probes. Most of the operational value
comes from disagreement; the detector prioritizes part of the residual blind
spot.

\subsection{What the supervised detector adds over a free alternative}
\label{sec:results:unsup}

The detector of Section~\ref{sec:method:direction} is fitted on labelled false
agreements. Section~\ref{sec:method:unsup} describes three scores that are not,
and comparing them bounds what the labels buy.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{llrrrr}
\toprule
& & \multicolumn{2}{c}{mass-mean probes} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{logistic probes} \\
\cmidrule(lr){3-4}\cmidrule(lr){5-6}
Method & Labels needed & AUROC & $>$ chance & AUROC & $>$ chance \\
\midrule
Supervised, fitted in-domain   & on the target     & $0.810$ & $100\%$ & $0.705$ & $96\%$ \\
Supervised, transferred        & other datasets    & $0.656$ & --      & $0.523$ & --     \\
\addlinespace
Label-free: margin             & none              & $0.674$ & $97\%$  & $\mathbf{0.780}$ & $99\%$ \\
Label-free: anomaly            & none              & $0.595$ & $61\%$  & $0.554$ & $53\%$ \\
Label-free: spread             & none              & $0.478$ & $39\%$  & $0.498$ & $47\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Detection AUROC by how the ranking is obtained, on the cells all
methods can score ($435$ for mass-mean, $330$ for logistic). ``$>$ chance'' is
the share of individual cells above $0.5$, which separates a score that works
everywhere weakly from one carried by a few datasets.}
\label{tab:unsup}
\end{table}

Two of the three label-free scores can be dismissed. \emph{Spread}, the gap
between the two probes' beliefs among claims where both say true, is at chance
($0.478$ and $0.498$) and below it in most cells: residual disagreement inside
an agreement carries no warning, which is worth recording because it is the
intuitive thing to try. \emph{Anomaly} is better than chance on average but
only in $61\%$ and $53\%$ of cells, and its aggregate is carried by one dataset
--- $0.855$ on Geometry of Truth against chance on IMDB and BoolQ. Unusualness
in the joint representation is not a general signal.

The \emph{margin} is a different matter. It beats chance in $97\%$ and $99\%$ of
cells, nearly as reliably as the supervised detector, and with logistic probes
it reaches $0.780$ --- above the supervised detector fitted on the same cells
with labels ($0.705$), and far above the same detector transferred from other
datasets ($0.523$). With mass-mean probes the ordering reverses and the
supervised detector wins by $0.136$. The two probes therefore disagree about
which method is better, and we report both rather than selecting.

The disagreement is interpretable. A logistic probe is a fitted classifier, so
its distance from its own decision boundary is a calibrated quantity; a
mass-mean probe is a difference of class centroids, and its belief is an
uncalibrated projection whose magnitude carries less. What does not change
across probes is the comparison that matters for deployment: the best label-free
configuration ($0.780$) is within $0.033$ of the best supervised one ($0.810$),
while a supervised detector transferred from other datasets is worse than either
($0.523$--$0.656$). At a $10\%$ review budget the margin reaches precision
$0.331$ against a base rate of $0.120$, so even the free ranking surfaces false
agreements at nearly three times the rate of random review.

Labels, in other words, are worth something only when they describe the
distribution being overseen. Labels gathered elsewhere do not transfer well
enough to beat a score that needs none --- and the distribution being overseen
is exactly the one the oversight problem assumes cannot be labelled.

\subsection{Probe depth}
\label{sec:results:depth}

Every result above reads the final layer. Truth directions are often reported as
strongest in middle layers, so we repeat the measurements at $0.5$ and $0.75$ of
model depth. Only the ten locally extracted models hold more than a final layer,
so this covers $49$ pairs rather than $128$, and a pair is dropped rather than
read at two different depths.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{llrrr}
\toprule
Measurement & Probe & $d=0.50$ & $d=0.75$ & $d=1.00$ \\
\midrule
In-domain detection & mass-mean & $0.829$ & $0.817$ & $0.795$ \\
                    & logistic  & $0.712$ & $0.731$ & $0.697$ \\
\addlinespace

                    & logistic  & $0.571$ & $0.599$ & $0.593$ \\
\addlinespace
Label-free margin   & mass-mean & $0.621$ & $0.683$ & $0.683$ \\
                    & logistic  & $0.745$ & $0.786$ & $0.778$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Detection AUROC by probe depth, paired per cell against the final
layer on the models that hold all three depths. Each column is restricted to the
cells its own comparison can score, so rows are not directly comparable across
depths without the paired differences quoted in the text.}
\label{tab:depth}
\end{table}

Depth $0.75$ is better than the final layer, modestly and not everywhere. The
supervised detector gains $0.023$ (mass-mean, $t=5.5$) and $0.034$ (logistic,
$t=5.2$) in-domain, and mass-mean gains $0.018$ on transfer ($t=3.8$). The
label-free margin gains almost nothing: $+0.001$ for mass-mean and $+0.008$ for
logistic. Depth $0.50$ is worse than the final layer on transfer and on the
label-free score for both probes, so this is a shallow optimum just before the
output rather than a general preference for middle layers.

The gain also costs something. Mass-mean false agreement rises from $0.122$ at
the final layer to $0.144$ at $0.75$ ($t=4.0$): the blind spot is larger there
and the detector is better at finding it. That trade-off has now appeared three
times --- with CCS probes, with base models, and with earlier layers. In each
case a weaker readout produces more shared error, and the shared error it
produces is more systematic and therefore easier to rank. It is a property of
the problem rather than of any one estimator.

\subsection{CCS on base models: a larger, still detectable blind spot}

CCS does not reliably read truth on base models: its belief AUROC is only
$0.527$--$0.548$. We therefore do not treat it as an unsupervised replacement
for the main probes. We can still ask whether a supervised third-stage detector
can rank false agreements produced by CCS probe pairs. Table~\ref{tab:ccs-base-models}
shows that detection AUROC is similar across the reported strata, while false
agreement rises from $0.151$ when neither model is a base model to $0.332$ when
both are. Across individual base models, detector AUROC ranges from $0.820$ to
$0.870$, overlapping the $0.804$--$0.850$ range for instruct models. The
observed result is therefore a larger blind spot with no obvious loss in
detectability for the supervised detector; it does not establish label-free
detection. These CCS cells are a non-random subset, and the two-base-model row
has only five cells, so the comparison is exploratory.
