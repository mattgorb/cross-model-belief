\begin{abstract}
Using one language model to monitor another's output has become the default
instrument of oversight: judges grade benchmarks, filter training data, supply
reward signal, and watch deployed behaviour. Every one of these uses rests on an
assumption that is almost never tested: that the judge fails differently from the
model it is judging. A judge that reads outputs is blind twice over. It cannot see
what the model keeps to itself, and it cannot see a mistake the two of them make
together.
%
Probes address the first blindness by not reading outputs at all. They recover a
truth direction from the model's internal state, so the judge sees what the model
represents rather than what it chose to say. Whether they address the second is
our question.
%
Fitting probes on two models and comparing their readouts, we characterize
\emph{false agreement}: both probes read a claim as true and the claim is false.
Its rate splits exactly into two parts, one set by how accurate the two probes
are and one by how correlated their mistakes are, and it can never exceed the
error rate of the better probe alone. Accuracy enters only the first part, so two
accurate judges that fail together are worse than two mediocre ones that fail
apart. We measure this across model pairs varying in family and scale, transport
probes with a linear alignment map, and report the cost of transport explicitly.
%
Across $49$ pairs spanning four families and $1.7$B to $32$B parameters, false
agreement accounts for $4$--$23\%$ of false claims and does \emph{not} fall when
the two models come from different families: the two parts cancel, because less
correlated mistakes arrive with higher individual error rates. Agreement between
internal beliefs therefore catches roughly half of the overseen model's errors,
and a direction fitted on the false agreements of four datasets recovers only five
further points of the remainder on a fifth.
%
The result bounds how much oversight agreement between models can provide, and
locates the limit in shared error rather than in judge accuracy.
\end{abstract}
