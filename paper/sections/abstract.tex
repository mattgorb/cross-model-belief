\begin{abstract}
Oversight by one language model of another assumes the two fail differently, and
a checker that reads outputs cannot test that assumption on anything the model
leaves unsaid. We therefore ask it of internal state: we fit a linear probe on
each of two models, read both on the same claim, and measure \emph{false
agreement}, where both probes report a claim as true and the claim is false. This
is the case disagreement-based oversight cannot see.
%
The rate is not free to vary. On the false claims it decomposes exactly into a
term set by the two probes' accuracies and a term set by the correlation between
their mistakes, and it can never exceed the error rate of the more accurate probe
alone. Accuracy enters only the first term, so two accurate checkers that fail
together are worse than two mediocre ones that fail apart.
%
Measuring this across $49$ model pairs from four families and $1.7$B to $32$B
parameters, on five datasets, we find false agreement on $4$--$23\%$ of false
claims, with positively correlated probe errors in nearly every pair. It does
\emph{not} fall when the two models come from different families: such pairs do
have less correlated mistakes, but they carry higher individual error rates, and
the two terms cancel. Transporting a probe between models through a linear map
costs almost nothing on some datasets and fails outright on others, so we report
that cost rather than assume it. Finally, the blind spot is largely invisible in
the activations that produced it: a direction fitted on the false agreements of
four datasets transfers to a fifth, but adds only five percentage points of
caught errors beyond what disagreement already catches on the dataset where false
agreement is most common, working well only where it is already rare.
%
Agreement between two models' internal beliefs therefore accounts for roughly
half of the overseen model's errors in our setting. The limit lies in shared
error, and is not removed by choosing a checker from a different family.
\end{abstract}
