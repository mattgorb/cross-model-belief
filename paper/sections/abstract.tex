\begin{abstract}
Oversight of one language model by another assumes the judge and the model being
judged fail differently. Yet an output-level judge cannot assess what the other
model leaves unexpressed, and agreement alone cannot distinguish joint
correctness from shared error. We test this assumption at the level of internal
readouts.
%
For two models presented with the same claim, we fit a linear truth probe to
each model's activations and define \emph{false agreement} as the event
that both probes classify a false claim as true. This is the blind spot of a
disagreement-based oversight system: neither reader raises an objection, but
both are wrong. We show that false agreement is determined exactly by two
quantities: how often each probe is wrong and how strongly their errors are
correlated. Fr\'echet bounds set the range of possible false agreement, with its
maximum fixed by the error rate of the more accurate probe. This separates two
properties that are often conflated when selecting a model judge: accuracy and
diversity.
%
Empirically, we measure both quantities using two families of supervised linear
probes across $571$ unique model-pair--dataset cells: $16$ open-weight models
from five families, spanning $1.7$B to $405$B parameters, evaluated on five
datasets. We then ask whether the blind spot is itself recognizable,
fitting a third linear classifier to distinguish false from genuine agreement,
and evaluating it on in-domain and out-of-domain data.
%
False agreement averages $2$--$25\%$ across datasets, and probe errors are
positively correlated in $84$--$98\%$ of cells. Additionally, we find that
choosing a judge from a different model family does not reliably reduce false
agreement under either probe. Although cross-family pairs have less-correlated errors in
aggregate, this difference disappears among similarly sized models. Finally,
we find that false agreement is strongly separable under in-domain evaluation, although the
learned detectors transfer unevenly to unseen data distributions, suggesting
that shared error leaves a detectable but largely domain-specific signature.
\end{abstract}
