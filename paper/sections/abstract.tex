\begin{abstract}
Using one language model to monitor another's output has become the default
instrument of oversight: judges grade benchmarks, filter training data, supply
reward signal, and watch deployed behaviour. Every one of these uses rests on an
assumption that is almost never tested, that the judge fails differently from the
model it is judging. It reads outputs, so anything the model does not express
escapes it, and any error the two share escapes it as well.
%
Linear probes answer the first objection. They recover a truth direction from
activations, giving a judge access to what a model represents rather than what it
reports. We ask whether they answer the second.
%
Fitting probes on two models and comparing their readouts, we characterize
\emph{false agreement}: both probes read a claim as true and the claim is false.
Restricting attention to the false claims and writing $p_i$ for each probe's
false-positive rate and $\rho$ for the correlation between their errors, the rate
is exactly $\FA = p_1 p_2 + \rho\sqrt{p_1(1-p_1)}\sqrt{p_2(1-p_2)}$, bounded above
by $\min(p_1,p_2)$. Accuracy enters only through the first term, so two accurate
judges that fail together are worse than two mediocre ones that fail apart. We
measure this across model pairs varying in family and scale, transport probes
with a linear alignment map, and report the cost of transport explicitly.
%
Across $49$ pairs spanning four families and $1.7$B to $32$B parameters, false
agreement accounts for $4$--$23\%$ of false claims and does \emph{not} fall when
the two models come from different families: the identity's two terms cancel,
because less correlated errors arrive with higher individual error rates.
Agreement between internal beliefs therefore catches roughly half of the overseen
model's errors, and a direction fitted on the false agreements of four datasets
recovers only five further points of the remainder on a fifth.
%
The result bounds how much oversight agreement between models can provide, and
locates the limit in shared error rather than in judge accuracy.
\end{abstract}
